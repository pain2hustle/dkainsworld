A LITTLE BUZZ FROM APIARY
  1. A waggle is a vector A returning forager dances on the comb in a figure-eight. The angle of the waggle relative to vertical maps to the angle of the food source relative to the sun.
  2. The brood has temperature control Honeybees regulate the brood nest near 35°C using muscle heat, fanning, and evaporative cooling. A broodless winter cluster is not held at one fixed temperature.
  3. Nectar becomes a concentrated food Honeybees add enzymes and remove water from nectar. Dry, sealed storage is central to how honey keeps.
  4. A queen lays her bodyweight in eggs each day Peak season: up to 2,000 eggs in 24 hours. The queen's only job is reproduction; she is fed and groomed by attendants for life.
  5. Workers are all female Every forager, nurse, guard, and undertaker in the hive is female. Males (drones) exist only to mate, and the colony evicts them before winter.
  6. The hive votes on a new home When a colony swarms, scout bees inspect candidate cavities and dance their findings on the swarm cluster. The dance gets stronger as more scouts agree. Quorum wins.
  7. A bee visits ~50 flowers per trip And makes 10-15 trips a day in peak season. That's hundreds of flowers per bee per day, millions per hive per season.
  8. One pound of honey = ~2 million flowers Honey is the compressed labor of a colony. The math of effort-to-product is brutal and beautiful.
  9. Bees see ultraviolet Flowers have UV nectar guides — landing-strip patterns invisible to humans. Bees see in trichromatic UV/blue/green, not red/green/blue.
  10. Honeycomb is mathematically optimal The hexagonal cell uses the least wax for the most volume — a 2,300-year-old conjecture (the Honeycomb Conjecture) finally proven by mathematicians in 1999.
  11. A hive is a superorganism No single bee is the colony; the colony is the unit of selection. Workers will die for it without hesitation. Apiary takes notes.
  12. Bees recognize human faces Honeybees can be trained to distinguish individual human faces, using the same configural processing humans use. With ~960,000 neurons.
  13. Propolis is the hive's immune system Bees seal cracks with propolis — a resin gathered from tree buds, mixed with wax and enzymes. It is antimicrobial, antifungal, and antiviral.
  14. Honey keeps well when sealed Low moisture and acidity help properly stored honey resist spoilage. Added moisture can allow fermentation; ancient-tomb edibility stories need evidence.
  15. Diet shapes queen development Different feeding regimes help female honeybee larvae develop into queens or workers. Male larvae follow a different developmental path.
  16. The colony can change your job Worker honeybees often shift from indoor tasks to foraging as they age. The sequence is flexible and responds to colony needs.
  17. A flower can release pollen when buzzed Bumblebees vibrate certain flowers with their flight muscles to shake out pollen. Honeybees do not perform this kind of buzz pollination.
  18. A colony plans for winter from spring Stores are built all season for a season the bees alive in summer will not personally see. Multi-generational savings, instinctively.
  19. The hive smells like home Each colony has a distinct chemical signature, and guards check incoming bees against it. Wrong scent → no entry. Apiary calls this the amygdala.
  20. Bees do math by smell Foragers integrate scent gradients, distance, and energy expenditure to decide whether a patch is worth returning to. Calorie-aware optimization.
  21. Wax is a secretion Young workers produce wax flakes from glands on their abdomen. They chew the flakes into shape with their mandibles. The architecture is literally their own bodies.
  22. Bees sleep They rest 5-8 hours a night. Sleep-deprived bees forage worse and dance less accurately — the same way you do worse work tired.
  23. Hives clean their own dead Specialized 'undertaker' bees remove corpses within hours, carrying them hundreds of meters from the hive. Hygienic colonies survive parasite pressure.
  24. Africanized bees defend more aggressively Not more venomous — more reactive. They mobilize more guards, respond at greater range, and pursue intruders farther. Same hardware, different governance.
  25. Bee colonies have democratic deadlocks too When swarm scouts can't agree, the swarm sometimes splits or returns to the parent hive. Decision-by-quorum has the same failure modes as any democracy.
  26. Pollination is the side effect Bees gather food. The flowers' DNA hitches a ride. A third of human food crops depend on this accidental ecosystem service — billions of dollars of unpaid labor.
  27. A hive can deliberate without a leader Queens don't command. They lay eggs. The colony's decisions — when to swarm, where to forage, how to defend — emerge from local rules and feedback. Apiary borrows the model.
  28. One early mating period, stored for years A young honeybee queen mates with multiple drones over one or more flights, then stores sperm to fertilize eggs over her reproductive life.
  29. Worker bees can lay eggs — but only male ones Unfertilized eggs become drones. A queenless hive in distress will see workers begin laying. The hive becomes male-only and dies out within months. No queen, no future.
  30. Bees have five eyes Two large compound eyes plus three simple eyes (ocelli) on top of the head. The ocelli sense light intensity and horizon — critical for navigation. Apiary respects sensors with redundancy.
  31. Honey is bee vomit, processed Foragers store nectar in a honey stomach separate from their digestive one. Back at the hive, they regurgitate it to house bees who chew it, add enzymes, and pass it bee-to-bee until ripe. Honey is a relay race.
  32. Some bees commit suicide for the colony When a bee is sick or parasitized, it sometimes voluntarily leaves the hive to die — a behavior called altruistic self-removal. Pathogen stays out. The colony's immune system has individual sacrifice built in.
  33. A drone has no father A drone develops from an unfertilized egg. His mother, the queen, had a father, so the drone can have a grandfather without having a father.
  34. Bees can recognize zero as a number Honeybees trained to pick the symbol with fewer dots correctly choose 'empty' over '1'. They grasp the abstract concept of nothing — a cognitive ability long thought to require a primate brain.
  35. The queen's pheromone is the hive's WiFi Queen Mandibular Pheromone signals 'queen present.' Workers groom her, lick it off, and pass it bee-to-bee within hours. The colony's mood, ovary development, and unity all hinge on this molecular broadcast.
  36. Hives execute their queens When a queen falters — failing pheromone output, weak laying — workers cluster around her, raise their body temperature, and 'ball' her to death by overheating. The constitutional check on a failing executive.
  37. Bees decide to swarm by argument Scouts dance for competing cavity options. Each scout escalates her dance only if she still believes after re-checking. Disagreement fades, consensus crystallizes, the swarm leaves. Apiary calls this committee work.
  38. Wax production costs eight times its weight in honey Workers must eat about 8 lb of honey to secrete 1 lb of wax. Every cell in the comb represents serious caloric investment. Construction is the most expensive infrastructure the hive runs.
  39. A bee can fly for 6 miles round-trip But efficiency drops fast past 2 miles. Foragers calculate expected return against fuel burned and abandon distant flowers as net-negative. They are running unit economics in real time.
  40. Drones have huge eyes — for one job A drone's compound eyes nearly cover his head, optimized for spotting a queen in the sky during the nuptial flight. He has no stinger, no foraging skills, no hive defense. Mate or die unmated.
  41. Bees can do mental rotation Show a bee a shape, then show it the same shape rotated, then show it a different shape. The bee picks the rotated original. They can mentally turn objects — a skill thought reserved for vertebrates.
  42. A hive's smell changes by season Spring hives smell of nectar and brood. Late summer hives smell of fermenting honey and propolis. A trained beekeeper can sniff at the entrance and diagnose the hive's state without opening it.
  43. Honeybees are not native to the Americas European colonists brought Apis mellifera to North America in the 1600s. Native bees existed (bumble, mason, sweat), but the honeybee is an immigrant species — and a critical one for modern agriculture.
  44. A worker bee lives 6 weeks in summer, 6 months in winter Summer bees burn out flying. Winter bees, born late, eat stores and conserve heat in cluster. Same species, two life-strategies — switched by the season and by glandular changes inside the hive.
  45. Bees can recognize Picasso vs Monet Trained honeybees can distinguish paintings by these two artists with ~80% accuracy and even generalize to new paintings by each. Visual abstraction is in their toolkit. With less than a milligram of brain.
  46. The waggle dance encodes calories per meter Stronger dances mean richer sources. Bees factor distance, sugar concentration, and travel time into the dance's vigor — broadcasting a multi-variable cost-benefit summary on the comb.
  47. Honey absorbs water from the air Honey is hygroscopic — pull the lid off a jar in a humid room and it will gain weight overnight. Bees fan the open cells to evaporate water down to ~18% before capping the cell with wax to seal it.
  48. Killer bees were a lab escape Africanized honeybees came from 26 Tanzanian queens accidentally released in Brazil in 1957. They hybridized with local bees and spread north — reaching Texas by 1990. The defensiveness is genetic, not learned.
  49. A swarm is more docile than a hive Swarming bees gorge on honey before leaving and have no brood or stores to defend. They cluster on a branch for hours, sometimes a day, while scouts vote. Calmest moment in a bee's life.
  50. The queen pipes before emerging A new queen still inside her cell makes a piping sound — a high-frequency chirp — and other queens still in cells answer with a quacking sound. The colony hears who is hatching first. Sound coordinates emergence.
  51. A queen's age is measured in pheromone Old queens make less mandibular pheromone. Workers detect the drop and quietly raise replacement queens in 'supersedure' cells. The transition is gradual and often peaceful — the old queen sometimes survives to old age in the same hive.
  52. Bees see flowers as runways Many flower petals have UV patterns invisible to humans — concentric rings, arrows, landing-strip stripes — that guide bees to the nectar source. The flower is advertising in a wavelength only its pollinator can read.
  53. Foragers report failure too A bee that tried a flower and found it empty does NOT dance. The silence itself is information. Negative results pass into the hive's collective memory by absence — Apiary's logs treat absence as data, too.
  54. A queen can pause her laying for winter When daylight shortens and temperatures fall, the queen reduces and eventually stops egg-laying. The cluster contracts, workers fan less, the hive enters a low-power mode. Apiary calls this graceful degradation.
  55. Bees groom each other to remove mites Some colonies have 'hygienic behavior' — workers detect Varroa mites on hivemates and chew them off. Mite-resistant breeds are selected for this trait. Mutual aid is colony immunity.
  56. Bees taste with their feet Tarsal sensilla on the legs detect sugar concentration the instant a bee lands. She knows whether the flower is worth her time before she's even unfurled her tongue. Sensory decisions in milliseconds.
  57. Queens emit pheromones from multiple glands at once Mandibular (mood), tarsal (footprint, anti-swarming), Dufour's (egg-marking), tergal (attractant). A queen is a chemical broadcasting station with at least six channels running in parallel.
  58. A sweet surprise changes a decision In experiments, bumblebees given an unexpected sugar reward approached an ambiguous-coloured flower sooner. Researchers described an emotion-like change, not an optimistic dance.
  59. Foragers retire when their wings wear out A bee literally flies herself to death — wings tatter, muscle fibers fail. The hive doesn't fire her; her body retires her by physically degrading. Foraging is a one-way contract with the colony.
  60. Bees can count to four In landmark navigation studies, foragers can use 'turn at the third tree' rules. They lose precision past four. Subitizing — instant recognition of small quantities — is conserved across very distant species.
  61. Comb is built downward, then outward Workers cluster, secrete wax, and chain together with their legs to form a hanging scaffold. They build cells under gravity, top first, then expand laterally. The architecture starts as biology hanging from biology.
  62. Bees observe each other to learn Naïve bees that watched experienced bees solve a string-pulling puzzle learned faster than bees figuring it out alone. Social learning of novel skills exists in an animal with 0.0001 of our neurons.
  63. Each hive has a unique odor 'doorman' protocol Guard bees at the entrance sniff every returning bee. Wrong-hive bees loaded with nectar may be allowed in (the hive will take the food). Empty wrong-hive bees may be killed on sight. Context modifies enforcement.
  64. Pollen is bee protein Nectar is carbs; pollen is the protein and lipid source. Larvae are fed a bee-bread made of fermented pollen, honey, and gland secretions. Lose pollen variety and the brood develops weaker. Diet diversity is colony resilience.
  65. Bees can dance about the future Scout bees inside a swarm dance about potential homesites — places the colony has never been. The dance encodes a place that doesn't yet exist for them. Symbolic abstraction in an insect.
  66. A hive can have two queens — briefly During supersedure, mother and daughter queens may coexist for days or weeks, sometimes laying side by side. Eventually the colony reduces to one. The handoff is a soft fade, not a hard cut.
  67. Worker policing kills rogue eggs When a worker (illegally) lays an egg, other workers detect it by pheromone signature and eat it within hours. Same hive, same family, but the colony enforces queen-only reproduction. Internal compliance, biologically encoded.
  68. Some bees never leave the hive Young workers cycle through indoor jobs — cleaning cells, nursing, comb-building, food-storing — for the first ~20 days of life. They only step outside when their flight muscles mature. The hive has a literal back office.
  69. Pollen gets packed for storage Workers pack collected pollen into cells with nectar or honey and secretions. This stored pollen, called bee bread, feeds the colony.
  70. A hive cleans its own air Workers stand at the entrance and fan with their wings to drive a directional airflow through the hive — fresh in one side, stale out the other. CO₂ rises near the brood and triggers more fanning. Active ventilation, no thermostat needed.
  71. Swarming divides a colony During reproductive swarming, the old queen leaves with part of the workforce while the original nest raises a replacement. Absconding means abandoning the nest entirely.
  72. Bees can return home from 8 miles away — once Released far from the hive, a forager will navigate back using sun-compass + landmarks + an internal odometer. But she only gets one chance — extended foraging at that range is energetically infeasible.
  73. Hives prepare for orphan-hood If the queen dies suddenly, workers detect the missing pheromone within hours and convert 1-3 of the youngest larvae into emergency queens by feeding them royal jelly. Continuity is engineered in.
  74. Bee colonies have personalities Some hives are calm; some are 'hot.' Some are explorers; some are conservative foragers. Researchers measure these traits as stable colony-level signatures. The hive has a temperament — and it's heritable.
  75. Drones are evicted by autumn As nights cool and stores must stretch, workers stop feeding drones, drag them to the entrance, and refuse re-entry. Drones starve outside. The colony does ruthless headcount accounting before winter.
